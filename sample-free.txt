I. The First Three Minutes, and the Long Dark After
---------------------------------------------------

[the first instant] There is no before. Time begins here, at the smallest
    interval that carries any meaning, and everything that will ever exist is
    packed into a region smaller than a proton, at a temperature hotter than
    any number that will later find a use.

[5e-36 seconds] Space stretches. Not the objects within it, but the distance
    between them, doubling and doubling again, faster than light is permitted
    to cross the gap, because the rule about light does not govern the stage
    on which it travels. In the time it takes for nothing at all to happen,
    the universe grows by a factor larger than the number of atoms it will
    ever contain.
Inflation ends. Whatever drove the expansion decays into a bath of particles,
and the universe refills with heat at the cost of its own strangeness. But the
process leaves fingerprints behind. The quantum jitter that existed when the
universe was small has been stretched to the size of the sky. Every galaxy
that will ever form is already sketched here, as a whisper of density 0.822
above the average, where our own measurements find 0.811.

[0.000 seconds] Quarks lose their freedom. Below two trillion kelvin they can
    no longer fly apart, and the plasma condenses into protons and neutrons
    the way steam condenses into rain. Matter and antimatter annihilate almost
    perfectly. Almost. For every billion pairs that cancel, one particle of
    matter is left standing, for reasons nobody has ever fully explained.
    Everything that is ever built gets built out of that remainder.

[3.0 minutes] Nucleosynthesis begins. Deuterium finally survives long enough
    to be built upon, and over about twenty minutes the universe fuses a
    fraction of its ordinary matter into helium, leaving hydrogen at 75% of
    the total. The deuterium-to-hydrogen ratio settles at 1.84e-5, lithium-7
    to hydrogen at 7.58e-10, with 7.48 baryons for every 10 billion photons.
    Then space expands past the density where fusion can continue and the
    process stops, permanently, with the periodic table three entries long.

[51 kyr] For fifty thousand years radiation has outweighed matter, and its
    pressure has smoothed flat every clump that tried to form. Now the balance
    tips. Matter wins, and for the first time gravity is allowed to keep what
    it gathers, at redshift 3405.

[365 kyr] The universe cools through three thousand kelvin and electrons, for
    the first time, are moving slowly enough to be caught. Every nucleus takes
    one. Within a few tens of thousands of years the fog of loose charge
    becomes clear neutral gas, and light, which has spent its entire existence
    being knocked sideways after a few steps, suddenly finds the road open in
    every direction at once. That light is still travelling, against an
    expansion that today proceeds at 60.2 km/s/Mpc. It is the oldest thing
    anyone will ever see.

[510 kyr] And then, nothing to see. The light of recombination reddens out of
    the visible and the universe goes dark: no stars, no galaxies, only cold
    hydrogen and helium drifting, and the slow, patient, invisible work of
    gravity pulling on ripples too faint to notice. This goes on for a hundred
    million years. It is the longest quiet the universe will ever have.

[175.9 Myr] Something gives. The first ripples finish their fall. At 175.9
    million years, clouds of around a million solar masses reach the density
    where they can hold themselves together, and molecular hydrogen, the only
    coolant this chemistry-poor universe possesses, lets them shed heat and
    keep shrinking.

II. First Light, and the Making of Everything Else
--------------------------------------------------

[175.9 Myr] The cloud falls in on itself and cannot stop. There is no carbon
    here, no oxygen, no dust: nothing that can radiate away the heat of
    collapse efficiently, so the gas stays warm, and warm gas will not break
    into small pieces. It comes down in one enormous body.
It ignites. After a hundred million years of absolute darkness there is a
star: 176 solar masses of it, burning 5621939 times brighter than the Sun
will, at 64424 K, a hard blue-white, painful to look at — a colour no eye
exists yet to be hurt by. The ultraviolet pours out and strips electrons off
the hydrogen for light years in every direction. First light.

[176.2 Myr] It runs out of hydrogen in 312 thousand years — less time than it
    took to form the cloud it was born in — and ends as a pair-instability
    supernova that leaves nothing at all behind.
Nothing survives it. The core got hot enough that photons began turning into
electron-positron pairs, the pressure holding the star up went with them, and
the whole thing collapsed and then detonated in one stroke. Every atom it made
is now moving outward at ten thousand kilometres a second. This is the single
most generous thing that ever happens in a universe: a star that keeps
nothing.

[225.9 Myr] The gas crosses a threshold nobody set. There are now enough heavy
    atoms — about 0.02 of a percent by mass — that dust grains can condense in
    cooling ejecta, and dust changes everything: it radiates heat away, so
    clouds can fragment, so stars can be born small, so stars can be born
    *slow*. And grains stick to grains. Rocks are possible now. [Fe/H] is
    -2.14.
Everything after this is downstream of that.

[4.876 Gyr] The gas reaches roughly the chemistry the Sun was born with. Iron
    has caught up with oxygen as the white dwarfs of earlier generations begin
    detonating: [O/Fe] has fallen to 0.30 from the alpha-rich values of the
    early galaxy. Phosphorus, which almost nothing makes and everything alive
    needs, is present at 1.6e-5 by mass.

[12.176 Gyr] Twelve billion years of this. 1.42 billion supernovae have gone
    off. 91% of the galaxy's original gas is locked into stars, 1.20e10 solar
    masses still drifts free, and what drifts is 1.9% heavy elements by mass —
    1.33 times the enrichment the Sun was born into.

III. Glouzounn — a M star born 4.25 billion years after the first light
-----------------------------------------------------------------------
A cloud a few light years across, cold enough that its hydrogen has paired
into molecules, gets nudged by a passing spiral arm, by the shockwave of a
nearby supernova, by nothing much at all, and part of it loses the ability to
hold itself up against its own weight.
It falls. Because it was turning, even slightly, it cannot fall straight:
angular momentum has to go somewhere, so the fall flattens into a disk with a
swelling knot at the centre. The knot climbs to ten million kelvin and
hydrogen starts fusing into helium. That is a star, and this one carries 0.34
solar masses, gives off 0.020 solar luminosities, and burns at 3313 K, which
is 3040 C. It is an M-type star, expected to linger 175.29 billion years on
the main sequence, with a metallicity of 0.012, about 0.88 times solar, and a
radius of 0.42 solar radii. From far enough away to survive looking, it reads
as a deep, dim red.
In the leftover disk, dust grains stick where they touch. Pebbles turn into
boulders, boulders into mountains, mountains into bodies with their own pull,
and once a body has its own pull it stops waiting for collisions to find it
and starts gathering them in. Ten million years later the gas is gone and 13
worlds remain.

IV. Planet g, and What Happened On It
-------------------------------------
The oceans are warm and the sky is carbon dioxide and nitrogen with no free
oxygen in it at all. Short-wavelength light from the parent star reaches the
surface unimpeded. At the sea floor, where water meets hot rock, there are
steep chemical gradients — protons on one side of a mineral membrane and not
the other — and a gradient is a battery. Molecules that happen to catch that
energy get to do more chemistry than molecules that do not.

[872.0 Myr] Something copies itself. Not well — the copy is wrong perhaps one
    time in ten — but wrongness is the point, because a copy that is slightly
    different is a thing that can be selected. The first lineage is called
    Seikrok here, for the sake of having something to call it. It is a single
    cell with 7 genes, it eats the gradient at the vents, and oxygen would
    kill it instantly if any existed.
From this point nothing needs to be explained again. Everything that follows
is this, repeated, under changing conditions.

[1.227 Gyr] An impact. 6 kilometres of stone and iron, delivering in about
    four seconds more energy than the planet's whole interior gives off in a
    century. The shock boils the shallow sea it lands in and the steam carries
    salt into the stratosphere, though nothing here yet lives on light.
50% of the lineages alive the year before are gone. What is left is small,
patient and unspecialised, and it inherits an empty world. Within twenty
million years the survivors have spread into every job the dead used to do,
and they look nothing like what they replaced.

[1.645 Gyr] A lineage stops waiting for the rock to feed it and starts taking
    energy directly out of sunlight. It is not efficient and it does not yet
    split water, but the supply is effectively infinite and it falls on
    everywhere at once instead of only on the vents. Life leaves the sea
    floor.

[1.774 Gyr] Something works out how to split water. Water is everywhere and it
    is a far better source of electrons than anything that came before, and
    the lineage that manages it inherits the ocean. There is a waste product.
    Oxygen is violently reactive and nothing alive can handle it, and for now
    it all goes straight into the iron dissolved in the sea, which rusts and
    sinks. The ocean floor turns red in bands. The air does not change at all.

[2.792 Gyr] The rust runs out. With nothing left to absorb it, oxygen goes
    into the air and stays there, and the air is now 2.5% oxygen and climbing.
    This is the worst thing that has ever happened to life on this planet.
    Almost everything alive is anaerobic and oxygen tears its chemistry apart.
    The methane that has been keeping the world warm is oxidised out of the
    sky, and the temperature falls.
And it is also the best thing that has ever happened, for the small number of
lineages that survive it. Burning food with oxygen releases roughly sixteen
times more energy than fermenting it. Everything expensive that life will ever
do — moving fast, growing large, thinking — is affordable only on this budget,
and the budget did not exist until life itself created it as a poison.

[2.795 Gyr] Enough oxygen has accumulated that a layer of ozone forms high in
    the atmosphere, and short-wavelength light at the ground drops to 26% of
    what it was. The land, which has been sterile rock and dust for the entire
    history of this world, stops being lethal. Within a few million years
    there is something growing on it.
Something had to learn to eat light. It had to learn to split water, and taint
the whole world doing it. The taint had to build up for a billion years until
it reached the upper atmosphere and became a shield. Only then could anything
crawl out onto the land. The continents were opened by a waste product.

[3.122 Gyr] Cells stop separating after they divide. At first it is only a
    clump, and the clump is harder to swallow than a single cell is, which is
    reason enough. Then cells in the middle find themselves in a different
    situation from cells on the outside, and start doing different things, and
    once that happens there is a body.

[3.124 Gyr] Popeotrix begins eating other living things instead of making its
    own food. This alters the rules for everything at once. Being large is now
    a defence. Being fast is now a defence. Being armoured, being hidden,
    being poisonous, being able to see what is coming: none of these were
    worth anything last epoch and all of them are worth everything now.
    Diversity does not increase gradually after this. It detonates.

[3.342 Gyr] A cell swallows another cell and does not digest it. The swallowed
    one is good at oxygen; the swallower is large and slow. Neither dies. They
    divide together, and keep dividing together, until there is no longer a
    sensible way to say there are two of them. The lineage is called
    Brubryuglauph and its genome has 32 genes, which is more than anything on
    this planet has ever carried. It is a cell with a power plant inside it,
    and it can now afford to be a hundred times bigger than its ancestors.

[3.356 Gyr] Some cells in some bodies specialise in carrying signals, and the
    bodies that have them can respond to the world faster than the world
    shifts. A net of them thickens at the end that usually goes first.

[3.998 Gyr] The ice sheets pass the latitude where the albedo feedback becomes

    ocean skins over. Beneath the ice, in the dark, at the vents, everything

V. The End of the Main Sequence
-------------------------------

[6.872 Gyr] The simulation arrives at the boundary of what was computed. The
    star continues fusing hydrogen and the world continues rotating on its
    axis. Nothing has finished; the line drawn here marks where the
    calculation stops. Running the program again with --persist would carry it
    onward, through the remaining phases of the star.

By the close of the main sequence, 260 lineages remain. A slice through them
is more useful than a list of the largest, since the leading entries would
repeat the same successful pattern half a dozen times.

Breistinn is the most abundant, accounting for 17% of the biosphere across 55
genes, having first appeared at 4.370 Gyr.
A lineage of dog-sized, swift predators that use oxygen, though wastefully,
while submerged in cold water, and that display the earliest scaffolding of
nervous tissue.

Chyulele is the largest by body size, making up 0.14% of the biosphere across
55 genes, with a first appearance at 5.785 Gyr.
Dog-sized, fast-moving hunters that draw on oxygen, though without great
economy, while moving through cold water, and that carry the rudiments of a
nervous system.

Treoso is the oldest lineage still in existence, occupying 0.22% of the
biosphere across 27 genes, having originated at 3.147 Gyr.
Single-celled and quick, subsisting on light and using oxygen, though with
poor efficiency, while drifting in cold water.

The trophic structure, measured by share of the biosphere, looks like this:
17% of organisms draw their energy solely from light or from rock chemistry,
almost none sustain themselves entirely by preying on other organisms, and the
remaining 83% combine both modes, which is the configuration this world has
arrived at. Almost nothing that is alive spends its life out of the water.
