I. The First Three Minutes, and the Long Dark After
---------------------------------------------------

[the first instant] Time begins. There is no earlier moment to refer to, no
    countdown, no approach. At the smallest interval that physics will later
    recognize as meaningful, everything that will ever exist occupies a region
    far smaller than a proton. The temperature has no name yet because nothing
    has cooled enough to define a scale.

[5e-36 seconds] At 5e-36 seconds, the space between things begins to stretch.
    Not the particles themselves but the distance separating them, doubling
    over and over, faster than light can cross the widening gap. This is
    permitted because the speed limit governs only what moves through space,
    not the expansion of space itself. By the time this surge ends, the
    universe has grown by a factor exceeding the total number of atoms it will
    eventually hold.
The inflationary expansion stops. Whatever field sustained it decays, and its
energy floods back into particles and radiation, reheating the universe at the
price of erasing the mechanism that drove the growth. But the expansion has
already stretched quantum fluctuations from subatomic scales to cosmic ones.
These ripples in density, one part in a hundred thousand above the average,
will later be measured at a depth of 0.822 in this universe, compared to the
0.811 observed in ours. Every galaxy that will ever coalesce is already
faintly written into the distribution of matter.

[0.000 seconds] At 0.000 seconds, the temperature drops below two trillion
    kelvin. Quarks, which have moved freely in the plasma, can no longer
    escape one another's grip and condense into protons and neutrons. Matter
    meets antimatter and the two annihilate with near-perfect efficiency. For
    every billion pairs that destroy each other, a single particle of matter
    persists. No one has yet explained why the symmetry broke this way.
    Everything solid, every star and planet and living thing, is made from
    that small surplus.

[3.0 minutes] At 3.0 minutes, nucleosynthesis begins. Deuterium nuclei, which
    formed and fell apart repeatedly in the earlier heat, now survive long
    enough to fuse into heavier elements. Over the next twenty minutes,
    roughly 25% of the universe's ordinary matter burns into helium, leaving
    75% as hydrogen. Trace amounts remain: 1.84e-5 deuterium nuclei per
    hydrogen nucleus, and 7.58e-10 lithium-7 nuclei per hydrogen. Then
    expansion carries the density below the threshold for fusion, and the
    process halts. The periodic table holds three entries. For every 10
    billion photons, there are 7.48 baryons.

[51 kyr] At 51 thousand years, the energy density of radiation, which has
    dominated since the beginning, falls below that of matter. Until now,
    radiation pressure has resisted the collapse of any overdense region,
    smoothing structure before it could grow. The balance tips. Matter takes
    control, and gravity is free to amplify the small differences in density
    that inflation left behind. The redshift is 3405.

[365 kyr] At 365 thousand years, the universe cools through three thousand
    kelvin. Electrons, which have been moving too fast to remain bound, slow
    enough for nuclei to capture them. Over the span of a few tens of
    thousands of years, ionized plasma becomes neutral gas. Light, which until
    now has scattered off free electrons every short distance, finds the
    universe suddenly transparent. Photons stream outward in every direction
    without interruption. The expansion rate today is 60.2 kilometers per
    second per megaparsec. That light has been traveling ever since and will
    be the oldest signal any observer can detect.

[510 kyr] Beyond five hundred and ten thousand years, the cooling makes vision
    impossible. Recombination's glow shifts past red into wavelengths no eye
    could use, and what remains offers nothing to see: hydrogen and helium
    adrift in darkness, no fusion yet, no luminous object of any kind. Gravity
    continues pulling on the irregularities seeded earlier, but the forces act
    slowly and leave no trace a passing witness could detect. This emptiness
    persists for a hundred million years—the longest span of quiet the cosmos
    will know.

[175.9 Myr] At one hundred seventy-five point nine million years, the first
    collapse completes. Clouds containing around one million solar masses,
    gathered at sites where the initial irregularities ran deepest, compress
    to the point where self-gravity can hold them intact. Molecular hydrogen,
    the sole chemical capable of shedding heat in a universe still bare of
    metals, allows these concentrations to cool and contract further. The
    redshift stands at twenty. Something has given way.

II. First Light, and the Making of Everything Else
--------------------------------------------------

[175.9 Myr] The cloud folds inward under its own weight and finds no way to
    halt. This gas holds no carbon, no oxygen, no grains of any kind—nothing
    to carry the warmth of infall away into space—and so it stays hot, and hot
    gas will not shatter into smaller clumps. The whole mass descends as a
    single object.
It ignites. One hundred million years after the last photon faded, there is a
star again: one hundred seventy-six solar masses of it, radiating 5621939
times the luminosity the Sun will one day have, its surface at 64424 K, a hard
blue-white that would pain any eye if one existed to see it. Ultraviolet
radiation streams outward and strips the hydrogen of its bound charge for
light years in all directions. This is first light.

[176.2 Myr] The hydrogen fuel gives out after 312 thousand years—less time
    than the cloud took to form—and the star dies as a pair-instability
    supernova that leaves no remnant.
What held the core up vanished when photons grew energetic enough to spawn
electron-positron pairs. The star fell inward and then exploded in a single
motion. Every atom forged in its interior now flies outward at ten thousand
kilometers a second. This is the most generous act the universe permits: a
star that retains nothing of itself.

[225.9 Myr] At 225.9 Myr, the dispersed material crosses an unwritten
    boundary. Heavy atoms now make up about 0.02 of a percent by mass, enough
    that dust grains can form in the cooling debris, and dust transforms what
    comes next. It radiates warmth into space, so clouds can splinter, so
    stars can form smaller, so stars can form slower. Grains adhere to one
    another. Solid bodies become possible. The iron-to-hydrogen ratio stands
    at -2.14.
All that follows descends from this.

[4.876 Gyr] At 4.876 Gyr, the interstellar medium has reached approximately
    the composition from which the Sun will form. Iron has risen to match
    oxygen as white dwarfs from older stellar populations detonate: the
    oxygen-to-iron ratio has dropped to 0.30 from the alpha-enhanced values of
    the young galaxy. Phosphorus, which few processes create and all life
    requires, is present at 1.6e-5 by mass.

[12.176 Gyr] Twelve billion years of this. One billion four hundred twenty
    million supernovae have detonated across the disk. Ninety-one percent of
    the galaxy's original gas now sits locked in stars. One point two times
    ten to the tenth solar masses still drifts free between them, and what
    drifts carries one point nine percent heavy elements by mass. Iron sits
    sixteen hundredths of a dex above the Sun's natal abundance. Oxygen runs
    twenty-three hundredths of a dex above iron, the signature of core
    collapse written into the gas. Rock-forming elements—silicon, magnesium,
    the builders of worlds—stand at one point one four times what the Sun knew
    at birth. The galaxy has grown metal-rich, patient, methodical, each
    explosion a small correction to the mixture.

IV. Planet g, and What Happened On It
-------------------------------------
The oceans hold heat and the air above them is carbon dioxide mixed with
nitrogen, no free oxygen present. Ultraviolet light passes through without
obstruction and strikes the water. Where ocean meets hot rock at depth,
minerals form barriers across which protons gather on one side and not the
other. The difference in concentration stores energy. Molecules that capture
this energy can drive reactions that other molecules cannot.

[872.0 Myr] At 872.0 Myr, something makes a copy of itself. The copy fails to
    match the original in roughly one case out of ten, but the errors matter
    because a variant can be tested against its parent. The first such lineage
    is named Seikrok here, to give it a label. It is a single cell carrying 7
    genes, it draws energy from the proton differences at the vents, and
    exposure to oxygen would destroy it immediately if any were present.
From this moment, no further explanation is required. All that comes after is
this same event, repeated, in different circumstances.

[1.227 Gyr] At 1.227 Gyr, something strikes the surface. The object is 6
    kilometres across, made of stone and iron, and it delivers in roughly four
    seconds more energy than the planet's entire interior releases in a
    century. The impact boils the shallow sea it strikes and steam lifts salt
    into the high air, though no organism yet harvests light.
Half of the lineages living the year before are gone. What remains is small,
unspecialized, and able to wait. It inherits a world emptied of competitors.
Within twenty million years the survivors have filled every role the extinct
once occupied, and they bear no resemblance to what they replaced.

[1.645 Gyr] At 1.645 Gyr, a lineage stops depending on rock to supply its
    energy and begins taking it directly from sunlight. The method is
    inefficient and does not yet break apart water, but the supply is
    effectively limitless and it falls on every surface at once rather than
    only at the vents. Life spreads away from the sea floor.

[1.774 Gyr] At 1.774 Gyr, something learns to split water. Water is abundant
    and it provides electrons far more readily than anything available before,
    and the lineage that achieves this spreads across the ocean. There is a
    byproduct. Oxygen reacts violently with nearly everything and no living
    thing can tolerate it, and for now it binds immediately to iron dissolved
    in the sea, which oxidizes and settles. The ocean floor acquires red
    stripes. The air remains unchanged.

[2.792 Gyr] The rust is exhausted. With no more iron to bind it, oxygen enters
    the atmosphere and persists there, lifting its proportion to 2.5% and
    continuing upward. For nearly all life on the planet, this marks the onset
    of a catastrophe without precedent. The vast majority of organisms are
    anaerobic, and oxygen disassembles their molecular machinery. The methane
    that has stabilized the planet's warmth is stripped from the sky by
    oxidation, and the mean temperature begins to drop.
For the few lineages that endure the transition, the same event opens a new
thermodynamic frontier. Respiration using oxygen yields sixteen times the
energy of fermentation for the same quantity of food. Every energetically
costly activity that will later emerge—rapid locomotion, large body size,
cognition—depends on this enlarged power budget, which did not exist before
life manufactured it as a toxin.

[2.795 Gyr] Oxygen accumulation advances until a zone of ozone assembles in
    the upper atmosphere, cutting ground-level ultraviolet to 26% of its prior
    intensity. The land surface, which has been bare rock and dust through
    every prior epoch, ceases to be lethal. Within several million years,
    something is growing there.
The sequence that enabled this deserves notice. An organism had to evolve the
ability to harvest photons. It had to develop a mechanism for splitting water,
poisoning the biosphere in the process. The toxin had to build up over a
billion years until it formed a high-altitude screen. Only after that could
anything colonize the continents. The land was made accessible by a metabolic
waste.

[3.122 Gyr] Cells cease to separate after division. Initially the structure is
    merely an aggregate, and such an aggregate is more resistant to predation
    than a solitary cell, which suffices as an advantage. Then cells in the
    interior encounter conditions distinct from those at the periphery, and
    begin to specialize, and once specialization occurs there is a body.

[3.124 Gyr] Popeotrix begins consuming other living things rather than
    synthesizing its own sustenance. This alters the selection pressures on
    every organism at once. Large size becomes a defense. Speed becomes a
    defense. Armor, camouflage, toxicity, and the ability to detect
    approaching threats—none held adaptive value in the previous epoch, and
    all become critical now. The diversification that follows is not gradual.
    It explodes.

V. The End of the Main Sequence
-------------------------------

[6.872 Gyr] At 6.872 gigayears the simulation stops. The star continues to
    fuse hydrogen in its core. The planet continues its rotation. This is not
    the death of the main sequence but the limit of what has been calculated.
    To follow the system past this point requires a new invocation with
    persistence enabled, one that will carry the model to stellar exhaustion.

The biosphere at this stopping point contains 244 distinct lineages. Any
selection would be partial. Choosing only the most massive or most widespread
would display the same adaptive strategy six times over. What follows is a
sampling across the range of what now lives.

Rousur makes up a quarter of all living mass. It holds 52 genes. It first
appeared when the star was 5.047 gigayears old. Its abundance reflects a
strategy that has proven durable across more than a gigayear of planetary
time.
It is large, comparable to a dog in mass. It moves with speed through the
water. It hunts other organisms. Its respiration uses oxygen but does so
without much efficiency, a constraint imposed by the low temperatures in which
it operates. It has begun to develop structures that transmit electrochemical
impulses, early forms of what might be called nerves.

Brewauzaest represents one tenth of one percent of the biosphere. It carries
59 genes. It arose at 6.782 gigayears, making it among the most recent of the
major forms. Its size exceeds that of all other lineages now alive.
It is large, comparable to a dog in mass. It moves with speed through the
water. It hunts other organisms. Its respiration uses oxygen but does so
without much efficiency, a constraint imposed by the low temperatures in which
it operates. It has begun to develop structures that transmit electrochemical

Kriaveheil accounts for twelve hundredths of a percent of all living mass. It
possesses 55 genes, the highest count recorded in the simulation. It first
appeared at 4.498 gigayears, making it older than Rousur and far older than
Brewauzaest.
The animal weighs what a dog weighs and moves quickly through the water
column. It hunts. The reactions that extract energy from oxygen proceed slowly
in the cold, badly suited to this chemistry, but they proceed. The animal has
early forms of what will be called nerves: cells strung along its length that
can pass signals from one end to the other faster than diffusion allows.

Leilyuyeo is the oldest lineage still living. It first arose at 3.100 Gyr. It
now makes up essentially none of the living mass on this world. Twenty-nine
genes separate it from the root of all other life.
The cell is alone and quick. It draws energy from light. It also burns oxygen,
though the chemistry works poorly in cold water and yields less than it might.
It lives where the temperature stays low.

Seventeen percent of the living mass on this world runs on light or on
reactions pulled from rock. Essentially none lives by eating other life alone.
Eighty-three percent does both. This is the arrangement the world has arrived
at. Three-tenths of one percent of everything alive exists above the
waterline.
